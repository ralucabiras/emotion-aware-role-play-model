\section*{Abstract} 
Difficult interpersonal conversations can be hard to prepare for, particularly when practising with another person feels uncomfortable or is not readily available. This dissertation presents AffectLab, a non-clinical application that enables private, repeatable rehearsal of such conversations. Users practise with a simulated conversation partner, receive feedback linked to their words, and revisit alternative responses. The implemented system combines structured scenarios with emotion recognition from conversational text and speech. Uncertain emotion predictions can inform limited, optional pacing adjustments, while deterministic rules govern scenario progression, outcomes, feedback, and safety. Fallbacks preserve the rehearsal workflow when optional services are unavailable.

The emotion models were evaluated on a four-class IEMOCAP benchmark using five speaker-independent folds, with classification performance and confidence calibration assessed separately. Adding preceding conversational turns increased text macro F1 from 0.6651 to 0.7332; combining contextual text and audio with equal weights raised it to 0.7883. However, the strongest classifier produced less reliable confidence estimates. Fitting fusion weights and calibration on validation data improved confidence reliability while retaining most of the classification gain. Policy cases, automated tests, and full-stack checks supported the specified dialogue, fallback, and recovery behaviour under the tested offline configuration.

In a supplementary single-arm pilot, 12 of 15 enrollees completed the protocol, while realism and feedback usefulness each had a median rating of 5/7. Required pilot tasks bypassed multimodal inference and affect-aware pacing, so these ratings describe only the baseline rehearsal workflow and provide no evidence about the acceptability or benefit of emotion-aware adaptation. The small, uncontrolled pilot also cannot establish causal benefit or real-world transfer.

The work demonstrates how affective models can support a structured rehearsal system while leaving dialogue decisions under explicit control. Broader use requires validation of model reliability and application behaviour in the intended deployment environment.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} affective computing; emotion recognition; multimodal fusion; conversational context; difficult-conversation rehearsal; human--computer interaction.
