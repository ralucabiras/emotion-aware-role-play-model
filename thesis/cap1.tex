\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap1}

\section{Context and Problem}
\label{sec:context-motivation}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Difficult interpersonal conversations arise in ordinary situations such as negotiating workload, maintaining a personal boundary, or expressing a relationship need. Although a person may know what they want to communicate, pressure and disagreement can make it harder to state a clear request, respond constructively, or reflect on alternative wording. Structured role-play offers a way to practise these skills before a real interaction, particularly when it provides clear objectives, resistance, reflection, and feedback.

Conventional practice may require another person, a facilitator, or a scheduled and potentially uncomfortable session. A digital system can instead provide private, repeatable access and preserve the same scenario for comparison between attempts. Language models can add conversational variety, but unrestricted generation is not a sufficient foundation for dependable practice: a model may leave its assigned role, vary the intended difficulty, or produce feedback unsupported by the user's words. Scenario progression, outcomes, assessment, and safety therefore require explicit control.

A second challenge concerns affect. Meaning can depend on preceding dialogue and on acoustic delivery as well as the current transcript, which motivates contextual and multimodal emotion recognition. Yet a benchmark label is not a direct measurement of a person's internal state. Predictions can be wrong, overconfident, or inconsistent across text and audio, particularly outside the data on which the models were evaluated. An application must therefore decide not only how to infer affective information, but when that information is sufficiently reliable and appropriate to influence an interaction.

This dissertation addresses the integration problem created by these requirements: how to combine contextual text and speech models with flexible role-play while keeping dialogue outcomes, feedback, safety, privacy, and failure handling predictable. AffectLab treats affect predictions as bounded evidence for modest and reversible pacing choices, not as authoritative psychological judgements. Deterministic policies remain responsible for scenario state, observable communication features, and safety precedence, while optional generative services may realise an already constrained response. Model performance, application conformance, and supporting user feedback are evaluated separately so that evidence from one level is not used to justify claims at another.

\section{Aim and Objectives}
\label{sec:dissertation-aim}
\label{sec:dissertation-objectives}

The aim of this dissertation is to design, implement, and evaluate AffectLab, an emotion-aware system for rehearsing difficult interpersonal conversations. The system combines structured role-play, contextual text and audio-based affect recognition, uncertainty-aware adaptation, and evidence-grounded feedback while preserving transparency, user control, privacy, safety, and deterministic fallback behaviour.

The objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item to establish, from relevant research, the design constraints for structured communication rehearsal, contextual and multimodal affect recognition, calibrated uncertainty, and responsible human--AI interaction;
    \item to develop and compare utterance-only text, contextual text, audio, and late-fusion models under a speaker-independent protocol, evaluating classification and probability quality as distinct concerns;
    \item to design and implement scenario-specific dialogue policies and a bounded adaptation policy in which model confidence, modality agreement, availability, user preference, and safety determine only permitted, inspectable actions;
    \item to provide evidence-linked feedback and reflective functions within a privacy- and safety-conscious application whose core workflow survives optional-service failure; and
    \item to evaluate model behaviour and integrated application quality with evidence appropriate to each claim, while reporting the small user pilot only within its non-adaptive scope.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The dissertation is guided by four research questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] \textbf{Does incorporating preceding conversational turns improve text-based emotion recognition compared with classifying each utterance independently?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] \textbf{Does combining contextual textual information with acoustic information improve emotion-classification performance compared with the individual text and audio modalities, and what trade-off exists between classification performance and confidence calibration?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] \textbf{How can uncertain multimodal affect predictions be integrated into a structured role-play dialogue policy while keeping the system's behaviour explainable, controllable, safe, and robust to disagreement or inference failure?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ4.}] \textbf{To what extent does the completed AffectLab application satisfy its functional, reliability, recovery, accessibility, responsiveness, and deployment requirements under reproducible engineering evaluation?}
\end{description}

\section{Approach and Contributions}
\label{sec:proposed-approach}
\label{sec:contributions}

AffectLab uses a layered architecture that separates affect inference, dialogue control, response realisation, feedback, and safety. The experimental pipeline compares an utterance-only text classifier with a model that adds preceding causal dialogue turns. A separate speech model provides acoustic evidence, and late fusion combines the modality distributions. Classification metrics establish predictive performance, while calibration measures assess whether confidence is suitable for operational thresholds. Speaker-independent session folds and paired dialogue-level comparisons keep the principal model comparisons aligned.

Within the application, a deterministic controller represents each scenario as stages, character behaviour, completion conditions, and unresolved outcomes. It selects the next conversational action from observable evidence rather than allowing a language model to decide whether the user has succeeded. An optional generative service may phrase that action more naturally, but validation and deterministic templates preserve the recorded decision when generation is unavailable or unsuitable.

Multimodal estimates enter the interaction through a restricted affect policy. Confidence, agreement between modalities, inference availability, eligibility, and an explicit user preference determine whether the system preserves baseline behaviour, offers a pacing choice, or permits a brief acknowledgement. Safety interruption takes precedence and does not depend on the emotion classifier. This separation also prevents affect predictions from determining scenario success, feedback scores, or participation decisions.

Feedback is derived from inspectable language features associated with each scenario, such as a clear request, maintained boundary, non-blaming statement, acknowledgement, or practical next step. The relevant conversational turns remain available as evidence. Replay, retry, branching, comparison, personal takeaways, and action cards support reflection on alternative responses rather than reducing practice to a single score.

Evaluation follows the same separation of responsibilities. The model study addresses context, modality complementarity, and calibration. Authored policy cases and automated checks address dialogue transitions, fallbacks, persistence, recovery, accessibility, responsive behaviour, and the complete application workflow. Local timing measurements characterise only the tested deterministic path. A small pilot supplies supplementary feedback on the baseline rehearsal workflow; because its required tasks did not exercise multimodal adaptation, it is not evidence that adaptation benefits users.

The dissertation makes five principal contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A controlled evaluation of causal conversational context and multimodal complementarity.} Utterance-only text, contextual text, speech, and fused configurations are compared on matched speaker-independent data, with dialogue-level uncertainty estimates.

    \item \textbf{An explicit separation between classification performance and operational confidence.} Predeclared fusion and validation-fitted calibration expose the trade-off between the strongest classifier and the probability estimates used by an interactive policy.

    \item \textbf{A bounded architecture for affect-aware role-play.} Deterministic dialogue and safety logic remain authoritative, while uncertain affect estimates and optional generation are limited to inspectable, reversible actions with defined fallbacks.

    \item \textbf{Evidence-grounded rehearsal and reflection.} Scenario-specific outcomes and feedback are connected to observable turns, and replay and branching allow alternative responses to be compared from the same conversational context.

    \item \textbf{A layered verification framework for an integrated application.} Model evaluation, policy conformance, application verification, local performance, and supplementary user feedback are kept distinct, making the scope of each resulting claim explicit.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these contributions concern the responsible integration of affective models into an implemented communication-practice system. They do not imply that AffectLab identifies a user's true emotion or that completing a rehearsal improves a real relationship.

\section{Scope and Dissertation Structure}
\label{sec:scope-limitations}
\label{sec:dissertation-structure}

AffectLab is intended for adults rehearsing ordinary difficult conversations. The implemented scenarios cover workload negotiation, personal boundary-setting, and expression of a relationship need. Text interaction is primary and voice input is optional. The affective component is limited to supervised categorical recognition from text and audio; it excludes video, physiological signals, multilingual inference, diagnosis, therapy, autonomous crisis assessment, and consequential decisions about another person. Emotion estimates are uncertain application inputs, not findings about an individual's internal state.

The empirical model claims are limited to the defined IEMOCAP protocol. Its ten actors, controlled English-language interactions, annotation process, and categorical labels do not establish generalisation across cultures, accents, devices, populations, or spontaneous application use \cite{busso2008iemocap}. Deterministic language rules are reproducible but cannot recognise every appropriate paraphrase and are not validated measures of general communication ability or psychological constructs. Optional providers introduce additional privacy and reliability boundaries, so the text-based fallback remains part of the intended system rather than an exceptional mode.

Application evaluation establishes behaviour only for the tested cases and configurations. It does not replace independent security review, target-environment load and recovery testing, live-provider evaluation, participatory accessibility work, or validation of trained inference under deployment conditions. The small, uncontrolled pilot concerns the non-adaptive baseline workflow and cannot establish causal benefit, sustained skill development, or transfer to real conversations. AffectLab is therefore presented as an implementation-complete, deployment-oriented research application whose wider use remains conditional on further technical, ethical, and human evaluation.

The remainder of the dissertation assigns each topic one principal location. Chapter 2 reviews the literature and defines the research gap. Chapter 3 presents the architecture, requirements, and design boundaries. Chapter 4 explains the technically significant implementation mechanisms. Chapter 5 defines the evaluation protocol and reports model, application, and pilot evidence together with threats to validity. Chapter 6 answers the research questions, states the contributions and limitations, and identifies future work.
