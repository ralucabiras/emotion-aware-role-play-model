\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap1}

\section{Context and Problem}
\label{sec:context-motivation}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Anticipating a difficult conversation can bring anxiety about confrontation or uncertainty about what to say. Someone who needs to negotiate workload, maintain a personal boundary, or express a relationship need may postpone the conversation, avoid it, or struggle to find words during disagreement. A clear intention can be difficult to express under pressure.

Private, repeatable rehearsal offers a space to try different wording before a real interaction. Structured role-play can provide an objective, resistance from a conversation partner, and feedback for reflection. Repeating a scenario allows alternative responses to be compared without arranging practice with another person or facilitator. AffectLab provides this communication practice; it does not diagnose or treat anxiety.

Dependable rehearsal also creates technical challenges. Language models can add conversational variety, but may leave their assigned role, vary the intended difficulty, or produce feedback unsupported by the user's words. Scenario progression, outcomes, assessment, and safety therefore require explicit control.

A second challenge concerns affect: preceding dialogue and acoustic delivery can change how a transcript is interpreted. Contextual and multimodal models may help, but their predictions can be wrong, overconfident, or inconsistent. The application must decide when this uncertain evidence is appropriate to use.

This dissertation addresses that integration problem: combining contextual text and speech models with flexible role-play while keeping outcomes, feedback, safety, privacy, and failure handling predictable. AffectLab confines affect predictions to reversible pacing choices under explicit policies. Model performance, application conformance, and supporting user feedback are evaluated separately.

\section{Aim and Objectives}
\label{sec:dissertation-aim}
\label{sec:dissertation-objectives}

The aim is to design, implement, and evaluate AffectLab as a structured rehearsal system that combines contextual text and speech recognition with bounded adaptation and evidence-linked feedback.

The objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.4em, parsep=0pt]
    \item to establish, from relevant research, the design constraints for structured communication rehearsal, contextual and multimodal affect recognition, calibrated uncertainty, and responsible human--AI interaction;
    \item to develop and compare utterance-only text, contextual text, audio, and late-fusion models under a speaker-independent protocol, evaluating classification and probability quality as distinct concerns;
    \item to design and implement scenario-specific dialogue policies and a bounded adaptation policy in which model confidence, modality agreement, availability, user preference, and safety determine only permitted, inspectable actions;
    \item to provide evidence-linked feedback and reflective functions within a privacy- and safety-conscious application whose core workflow survives optional-service failure; and
    \item to evaluate model behaviour and integrated application quality with evidence appropriate to each claim, while reporting the small user pilot only within its non-adaptive scope.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The dissertation is guided by four research questions:

\begin{description}[itemsep=0.25em, topsep=0.4em, parsep=0pt]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] \textbf{Does incorporating preceding conversational turns improve text-based emotion recognition compared with classifying each utterance independently?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] \textbf{Does combining contextual textual information with acoustic information improve emotion-classification performance compared with the individual text and audio modalities, and what trade-off exists between classification performance and confidence calibration?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] \textbf{How can uncertain multimodal affect predictions be integrated into a structured role-play dialogue policy while keeping the system's behaviour explainable, controllable, safe, and robust to disagreement or inference failure?}

    \item[\textbf{RQ4.}] \textbf{To what extent does the completed AffectLab application conform to its functional, reliability, recovery, accessibility, responsiveness, and deployment requirements under documented engineering evaluation?}
\end{description}

\section{Approach and Contributions}
\label{sec:proposed-approach}
\label{sec:contributions}

AffectLab combines matched speaker-independent model experiments with an implemented rehearsal application. The experiments compare utterance-only text, causal conversational context, speech, and late fusion, assessing classification and calibration separately. The application separates model evidence from scenario decisions and links feedback to recorded turns. Chapters~\ref{chap:proposed-system-architecture} and~\ref{chap:implementation} explain the control boundaries and mechanisms, including replay and branching for comparing alternative responses.

Evaluation follows the same separation of responsibilities. The model study addresses context, modality complementarity, and calibration. Authored policy cases and automated checks address dialogue transitions, fallbacks, persistence, recovery, accessibility, responsive behaviour, and the complete application workflow. Local timing measurements characterise only the tested deterministic path. A small pilot supplies supplementary feedback on the baseline rehearsal workflow; because its required tasks did not exercise multimodal adaptation, it is not evidence that adaptation benefits users.

The dissertation makes three connected contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.4em, parsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Empirical: context, multimodal complementarity, and calibration.} Matched speaker-independent comparisons and dialogue-level uncertainty estimates establish the effects of causal context and text--audio fusion, while distinguishing classification performance from operational confidence.

    \item \textbf{Architectural: bounded affect-aware rehearsal and reflection.} Deterministic dialogue and safety policies constrain uncertain predictions and optional generation. Outcomes and feedback link to observable turns, while replay and branching support comparison from shared context.

    \item \textbf{Methodological: layered verification of an integrated application.} Model evaluation, policy conformance, application verification, local performance, and supplementary user feedback remain distinct, making the scope of each claim explicit.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these contributions concern the responsible integration of affective models into an implemented communication-practice system. They do not imply that AffectLab identifies a user's true emotion or that completing a rehearsal improves a real relationship.

\section{Scope and Dissertation Structure}
\label{sec:scope-limitations}
\label{sec:dissertation-structure}

Six built-in application scenarios support adults rehearsing ordinary difficult conversations; workload, boundary, and relationship practice use the enhanced staged policies evaluated here. Text interaction is primary and voice input is optional. The affective component is limited to supervised categorical recognition from text and audio; it excludes video, physiological signals, multilingual inference, diagnosis, therapy, autonomous crisis assessment, and consequential decisions about another person. Emotion estimates are uncertain application inputs, not findings about an individual's internal state.

Model claims concern the defined IEMOCAP protocol \cite{busso2008iemocap}; application claims concern observed behaviour under documented test conditions. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-limitations} sets out the statistical, construct, and deployment limitations.

Application evaluation establishes behaviour only for the tested cases and configurations. It does not replace independent security review, target-environment load and recovery testing, live-provider evaluation, participatory accessibility work, or validation of trained inference under deployment conditions. The small, uncontrolled pilot concerns the non-adaptive baseline workflow and cannot establish causal benefit, sustained skill development, or transfer to real conversations. AffectLab is therefore presented as an implementation-complete, deployment-oriented research application whose wider use remains conditional on further technical, ethical, and human evaluation.
