\chapter{State of the Art}\label{chap2}

Emotion-aware communication rehearsal combines several research traditions whose evidence should not be conflated. Communication-training studies can establish the value of structured practice, conversational-agent research can inform interaction design, and affective-computing benchmarks can measure agreement with operational emotion labels. None of these alone demonstrates that an integrated application is educationally effective, reliable for a new speaker, or safe. This chapter therefore reviews only the literature needed to motivate the research questions and design choices. It distinguishes throughout between predicting a dataset label, controlling application behaviour, and improving a person's communication skills.

\section{Communication Rehearsal, Role-Play, and Feedback}
\label{sec:sota-rehearsal}
\label{sec:sota-feedback}

Role-play turns interpersonal knowledge into observable decisions: a learner must formulate a request, respond to resistance, clarify an intention, and decide whether to maintain or revise a position. Compared with a consequential real interaction, a simulation permits mistakes, repetition, and reflection. Its value nevertheless depends on its learning objectives and feedback, not simply on conversational realism. In a randomised study of 103 medical students, Bosse et al.\ found that peer role-play and standardised-patient training improved communication performance and self-efficacy relative to a control condition \cite{bosse2012roleplay}. A systematic review of 14 virtual-patient studies similarly associated stronger interventions with preparation, scaffolding, debriefing, reflection, and feedback, while finding heterogeneous evidence for comparative effectiveness \cite{lee2020virtualpatients}. These clinical results support structured rehearsal but do not establish equivalent transfer to everyday workplace or relationship conversations.

Language models can broaden the range of situations available for rehearsal. IMBUE combines personalised interpersonal simulations with just-in-time, framework-based feedback; its randomised evaluation with 86 participants reported gains in measures including self-efficacy and skill mastery, but transfer to new and more difficult situations was more limited than improvement within trained situations \cite{lin2024imbue}. The result is important because it treats an LLM interaction as a learning activity rather than an open-ended chatbot. It also shows why performance inside a familiar scenario, perceived realism, immediate confidence, and transfer are separate outcomes.

Feedback is most actionable when it relates a learning goal to observable performance and a feasible next step \cite{hattie2007feedback}. In communication rehearsal, evidence might include whether the learner made a specific request, gave a reason, acknowledged another perspective, or maintained a stated boundary. Automated evidence remains a proxy: a lexical or rule-based detector can miss a valid paraphrase or misread quotation, negation, and sarcasm, whereas a fluent generated explanation can sound authoritative without being valid. Traceable feedback should therefore identify the turn that triggered an assessment and permit review, retry, or comparison of alternative continuations. Such mechanisms support reflection, but their presence does not prove improvement outside the system. This literature motivates defined scenario goals, legitimate unresolved outcomes, repeatable attempts, and evidence-linked feedback, while requiring any learning claim to be evaluated independently.

Repeatability creates an additional evaluation risk: learners may discover the phrases rewarded by one scenario without acquiring a strategy that survives changed wording, resistance, or context. Evidence recorded inside the simulator should therefore be distinguished from independent assessment and from transfer to an unfamiliar conversation. Self-reported confidence can describe a participant's immediate experience, but without a suitable comparator it cannot identify whether practice, feedback, novelty, or expectation produced the change. This distinction is particularly important for short pilots, which are useful for finding workflow problems and estimating acceptability but rarely support durable learning claims.

\section{Deterministic and Generative Conversational Agents}
\label{sec:sota-dialogue}
\label{sec:sota-affective-dialogue}

Classical task-oriented dialogue systems make conversational state and action selection explicit. RavenClaw, for example, separates domain-specific task logic from reusable dialogue-management capabilities such as turn-taking and recovery \cite{bohus2009ravenclaw}. For rehearsal, explicit states can represent raising an issue, clarifying a concern, responding to resistance, and reaching either a completed or unresolved outcome. Their principal benefit is controllability: transitions and permissible endings can be explained and tested. Their costs are policy authoring, incomplete coverage of natural phrasing, and repetitive surface responses.

Large language models provide more flexible linguistic realisation and can respond to inputs not anticipated in a template. Research on LLM role-play shows how personas, dialogue goals, and recovery strategies can structure multi-turn simulation \cite{tamoyan2025roleplay}. Flexibility is not the same as control, however. In a comparison of generated and human-authored professional role-play responses, Lu et al.\ found that judged quality deteriorated across turns in the evaluated setting, particularly for naturalness and context maintenance \cite{lu2025roleplay}. A plausible reply can also damage a rehearsal exercise by dropping a scenario constraint, becoming prematurely agreeable, or declaring success before its conditions have been met.

A hybrid therefore offers a defensible division of responsibility: deterministic policy selects the stage, resistance level, completion status, and allowed response plan; optional generation varies wording within that plan; and a deterministic response remains available if generation fails. Related work on emotionally conditioned and empathetic generation shows that affect-related signals can influence perceived response quality \cite{zhou2018ecm,rashkin2019empathetic}, but perceived empathy neither verifies the inferred emotion nor establishes an appropriate instructional action. In a practice scenario, scepticism or refusal may be intentional. Affect-related adaptation should consequently remain subordinate to scenario goals, user choice, and independent safety rules.

\section{Contextual Text and Speech Emotion Recognition}
\label{sec:sota-affect-theory}
\label{sec:sota-datasets}
\label{sec:sota-text}
\label{sec:sota-speech}

Emotion recognition requires an operational target. Categorical systems assign labels such as anger, happiness, sadness, and neutral, whereas dimensional accounts describe affect using quantities such as valence and arousal \cite{ekman1992basic,russell1980circumplex}. These representations are useful abstractions rather than direct measurements of private experience. Emotion, mood, feeling, expressive behaviour, and an annotator's interpretation are not interchangeable constructs \cite{scherer2005emotions}; observable cues also vary with speaker, culture, setting, and communicative purpose. Accordingly, ``emotion recognition'' here means prediction of annotated expressive categories. It does not license diagnosis, inference of intent, or a claim that the system knows what a user feels.

The Interactive Emotional Dyadic Motion Capture database (IEMOCAP) provides about twelve hours of scripted and improvised dyadic interactions from ten actors in five sessions, with speech, transcripts, other modalities, and emotion annotations \cite{busso2008iemocap}. Its paired modalities and conversational form make it suitable for controlled text--audio comparison. A common four-class task uses anger, happiness (often including excitement), sadness, and neutral, although published work differs in label mapping, preprocessing, splits, and metrics \cite{li2022contextaware}. Other conversational corpora, including the multi-party television dialogue in MELD and the written exchanges in DailyDialog, provide useful but non-equivalent domains \cite{poria2019meld,li2017dailydialog}. A catalogue of datasets adds little to the present argument: the important point is that results are comparable only under compatible protocols, and an acted English corpus with ten speakers cannot establish robustness across populations, devices, languages, or spontaneous deployment speech.

Conversational samples also violate the assumptions behind an utterance-level random split. Adjacent turns share speakers, recording conditions, subject matter, and affective trajectories. Holding out an entire IEMOCAP session prevents the same actors from appearing in training and test data, while a separate session for validation can protect model selection from test leakage. It does not remove the shared studio, task, and annotation conventions. Statistical comparisons should also respect dependence within dialogues rather than treating every utterance as an independent participant.

Protocol comparability extends beyond the split. Merging excitement with happiness, discarding unmatched utterances, balancing classes, or reporting weighted rather than unweighted measures can materially change the apparent result. A strong published score is therefore context for method selection, not a target that can be compared without reconstructing its data definition. The dissertation instead fixes one primary task and uses identical held-out items for the comparisons that answer its research questions.

Pre-trained text encoders such as BERT and RoBERTa provide contextual token representations that can be adapted to classification \cite{devlin2019bert,liu2019roberta}. Yet an utterance such as ``That's fine'' remains ambiguous without its conversational setting. Emotion-recognition-in-conversation models address this limitation through speaker states, dialogue graphs, commonsense features, or representations of a speaker's previous utterances \cite{majumder2019dialoguernn,ghosal2019dialoguegcn,ghosal2020cosmic,lee2022compm}. These studies motivate the use of dialogue history, but many offline arrangements are unsuitable for live interaction. A deployable classifier for turn $t$ may use the current utterance and a bounded history,
\[
  \hat{y}_t=f(u_t,u_{t-1},\ldots,u_{t-k}),
\]
but not future turns or neighbouring gold labels. A bidirectional token encoder can still be used inside this causally assembled input; bidirectionality over its tokens does not imply access to the future conversation. A matched comparison of utterance-only and causal-context models consequently answers a clearer question than an uncontrolled comparison among unrelated architectures.

Speech contributes pitch, intensity, timing, rate, voice quality, and pauses that a transcript omits, but these cues also reflect identity, health, language, microphone, and environment. Compact descriptors such as GeMAPS offer interpretable acoustic baselines \cite{eyben2016gemaps}. Self-supervised encoders including wav2vec~2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM instead learn representations from large speech collections \cite{baevski2020wav2vec,hsu2021hubert,chen2022wavlm}. Their success in speech representation learning is not itself evidence of emotion-classification accuracy, and a downstream model can still exploit speaker or recording artefacts. Speaker-independent evaluation is therefore essential. At application level, audio must also be optional and recoverable because permissions, noise, silence, short recordings, and the identifying content of a voice introduce failure and privacy costs absent from transcript-only inference.

\section{Multimodal Fusion, Calibration, and Uncertainty}
\label{sec:sota-fusion}
\label{sec:sota-calibration}

Text and speech provide complementary but differently structured evidence. Multimodal research distinguishes representation, alignment, and fusion as separate challenges \cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Early and cross-modal methods can model fine-grained interactions; for example, the Multimodal Transformer uses directional attention between unaligned streams \cite{tsai2019mult}. Such capacity also increases coupling and makes missing inputs and decision provenance harder to handle. For the present research questions, decision-level fusion supplies a more transparent test. If $p_T(y)$ and $p_A(y)$ are independently produced text and audio distributions, their mixture is
\[
  p_F(y)=\lambda p_T(y)+(1-\lambda)p_A(y), \qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
\]
Equal weighting predeclares $\lambda=0.5$; a fitted alternative must estimate it using validation data rather than the final test set. The rule is modular, exposes modality disagreement, and permits text-only fallback. It is less expressive than cross-modal attention and can still become confidently wrong when both branches exploit misleading cues.

Classification quality and probability quality answer different questions. Accuracy and macro F1 assess predicted labels, while calibration asks whether confidence corresponds to observed correctness under the evaluated distribution. Neural classifiers are often miscalibrated; temperature scaling can adjust logit magnitudes using a held-out validation set without changing the usual model's top-class ordering \cite{guo2017calibration}. More flexible multiclass methods such as Dirichlet calibration are possible, but require sufficient validation evidence and can overfit \cite{kull2019dirichlet}. Expected calibration error offers a useful summary but is bin-dependent, so it should be interpreted beside proper scoring measures or reliability evidence rather than as a complete guarantee.

Fusion makes this separation particularly important: the configuration with the strongest classification score need not have the best calibrated confidence. Training, weight selection, calibration fitting, and testing must therefore remain separated inside each speaker-held-out fold. Moreover, calibration is distribution-dependent and can degrade under shift \cite{ovadia2019shift}. A confidence threshold derived from IEMOCAP is an operational presentation rule, not a psychological boundary. Modality agreement is likewise informative but not proof. These limitations support restrained application behaviour: uncertain, conflicting, or unavailable evidence should lead to baseline dialogue or an explicit pacing choice, not to an imposed emotional interpretation.

\section{Research Gap and Design Implications for AffectLab}
\label{sec:sota-ethics}
\label{sec:sota-evaluation}
\label{sec:sota-synthesis}
\label{sec:sota-gap}

Prior work establishes the components of the problem but not their complete evidential chain. Communication-training research supports structured practice, reflection, and feedback; contextual and multimodal models can improve benchmark label prediction; calibration research qualifies the meaning of confidence; and generative agents provide flexible dialogue with known multi-turn control risks. What remains is an integration-and-evaluation gap: a bounded rehearsal system must connect these components without treating benchmark accuracy as emotion understanding, fluent generation as policy compliance, application tests as educational validation, or immediate user ratings as evidence of transfer.

This gap yields four design implications. First, model comparisons require a common, speaker-independent protocol. The contextual text model should be compared with an utterance-only counterpart on the same held-out dialogues, and a separately trained speech branch and transparent fusion rule should be evaluated on matched text--audio cases. Classification and calibration must be reported separately, with test data excluded from model and policy selection. These choices provide evidence for the first two research questions while keeping conclusions specific to the benchmark.

Second, affect inference should be advisory rather than authoritative. Its permitted effects should be few, reversible, and inspectable; scenario progression, completion, feedback, and safety remain governed independently. This boundary is especially important because emotionally expressive input can occur during serious distress. Risks identified for generative wellness applications justify clear scope limits and a safety route that does not depend on a four-class emotion model \cite{defreitas2024wellness}. In the European legal context, the Artificial Intelligence Act also restricts particular emotion-inference uses in workplace and educational settings, subject to its stated scope and exceptions \cite{eu2024aiact}. Voluntary personal rehearsal is not equivalent to institutional surveillance, but deployment would still require case-specific legal, privacy, and ethical assessment. Explicit microphone activation, minimised audio retention, access control, and withdrawal procedures follow from this boundary rather than from a claim of compliance by design alone.

Third, hybrid dialogue should keep control decisions separate from surface wording. Explicit scenario states and completion rules make intended resistance, unresolved outcomes, safety precedence, and generator fallback testable. Optional generation can improve variation but cannot alter these decisions. Evidence-linked feedback similarly remains an auditable implementation construct, not a validated measure of communication competence. These constraints turn the third research question into a question about specified application behaviour rather than machine empathy or psychological validity.

Finally, evaluation must match each claim. Model experiments assess annotated-label prediction and calibration; authored policy cases assess rule conformance; application tests assess functional, persistence, recovery, accessibility, interface, and performance behaviour; and a limited pilot can describe completion and participant perceptions. Passing one layer cannot substitute for another. In particular, a small, single-arm pilot cannot establish that affect adaptation caused improvement or that learning transfers to real conversations. This layered strategy addresses the fourth research question while preserving the distinction between technical verification and human benefit.

The resulting position is deliberately narrower than a new universal emotion model or an unconstrained role-playing companion. AffectLab investigates whether causal conversational context and text--audio fusion improve a defined speaker-independent task, whether probability quality changes the operational interpretation of those predictions, and whether a complete rehearsal application can apply bounded adaptation while remaining testable under failure. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-system-architecture} translates these implications into authority boundaries and requirements; Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} describes the mechanisms; and Chapter~\ref{chap5} evaluates each evidence layer.
