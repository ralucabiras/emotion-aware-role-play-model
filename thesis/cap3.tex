\section{Architecture and Design Principles}
\label{sec:system-overview}

AffectLab is a text-first application for rehearsing difficult interpersonal conversations. Its architecture combines deterministic application logic, trained affect-recognition models, optional generative services, and a browser interface, but it is not an end-to-end conversational model. The central design principle is separation of authority: probabilistic components may provide affective evidence or flexible wording, whereas explicit policies retain control of safety, dialogue progression, completion, and feedback evidence. This division makes consequential decisions inspectable and allows the core rehearsal to continue when a model or provider is unavailable.

Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture} shows the resulting information and authority flow. The interaction layer supports scenario selection, text and optional voice input, conversation display, and reflection. The application layer authenticates the request, validates ownership, coordinates the active session, and persists its state. Within the decision boundary, safety and dialogue policies are authoritative; affect inference supplies bounded evidence to the dialogue policy but cannot replace either authority. Response realisation converts a selected action into text using a constrained generator or its deterministic template. Feedback and reflection operate on recorded policy evidence rather than on unverified generated claims.

The architecture is deliberately text-first. Every conversational turn contains text, either typed by the user or reviewed after optional transcription. Text therefore remains available to the safety and dialogue components even when microphone permission is denied, audio is invalid, trained inference cannot be loaded, or an external provider fails. Raw audio is processed transiently and is not retained as part of conversation history. Optional services can improve modality coverage or linguistic variety, but their loss must not remove the core workflow.

The three standard scenarios---workload negotiation, personal boundary-setting, and expression of a relationship need---share a structural contract. Each defines its objective, stages, difficulty-dependent resistance, observable communication features, response actions, and possible outcomes. A deterministic policy evaluates the current message against that contract and records both the selected action and its evidence. The response-realisation layer receives the action only after this decision; consequently, generated wording cannot redefine success, remove intended resistance, alter the evidence record, or override a safety interruption.

Persistence stores the textual turns and enough decision metadata to reconstruct the interaction: state transitions, detected features, selected actions, reason codes, affect-policy decisions, component versions, and generation or fallback provenance. Completed sessions can then support evidence-linked feedback, replay, retry, branching, comparison, personal takeaways, and action cards. Research records form a separate, minimised projection rather than a copy of the participant-facing conversation.

Component boundaries are expressed through narrow contracts rather than shared access to model or provider internals. Inference returns probabilities and provenance; the affect policy returns a permitted adaptation decision; the dialogue policy returns state, evidence, and an action; and response realisation returns wording plus its source. Explicit unavailable and rejected states are part of these contracts. This makes failure behaviour testable, prevents an optional component from silently acquiring authority, and permits models, persistence implementations, or external providers to be replaced without rewriting the scenario policies.

These boundaries align the architecture with the research questions. Contextual text, speech, fusion, and calibration support RQ1 and RQ2; the separation of affect evidence from dialogue and safety authority addresses RQ3; and the integrated, recoverable application provides the object evaluated for RQ4. Model accuracy, policy conformance, application quality, and pilot feedback therefore remain distinct forms of evidence.

The groups also distinguish core from optional capability. Authentication, text interaction, scenario policy, safety handling, deterministic response templates, evidence-based feedback, and session management form the core. Voice capture, transcription, trained multimodal inference, generative wording, and research participation are optional. A valid implementation may expose an optional feature only when its configuration and failure path preserve the core requirements.

Consolidation does not weaken the atomic acceptance criteria. Each identifier still denotes a separately testable obligation, while a group states the common architectural boundary through which those obligations are implemented. Chapter~\ref{chap5} therefore traces identifiers to evidence rather than treating a group as a single pass/fail claim. This is particularly important for optional features: successful text fallback does not prove trained-inference quality, and successful provider generation does not replace verification of the underlying deterministic action.

\section{Dialogue, Affect Adaptation, and Safety Boundaries}
\label{sec:dialogue-affect-safety-boundaries}

Each turn follows an authority-ordered pipeline. Authentication, ownership, input, and stale-state checks occur first. The safety layer then examines the text before affect adaptation or response generation. If a supported crisis-related expression is detected, ordinary role-play stops, a predefined response is returned, and the interruption is recorded. Safety does not use the four-class emotion classifier: a neutral prediction cannot cancel explicit textual evidence, and a generator cannot suppress the interruption.

When no interruption is required, contextual text inference uses the current utterance and a fixed causal window of preceding turns. If valid speech is available, the audio model produces a separate distribution; calibrated modality outputs may then be combined through the declared late-fusion configuration. Availability, confidence, agreement, and component versions remain visible to the policy. Missing audio is absence of evidence, not evidence about the user's affect.

The affect policy maps this information to a small set of reversible actions. Depending on eligibility, user preference, dialogue state, confidence, and modality agreement, it preserves baseline behaviour, offers a pacing choice, or permits a tentative acknowledgement. It cannot determine crisis handling, scenario completion, feedback scores, study eligibility, or research inclusion. Thresholds are operational settings rather than psychological boundaries, and the interface must not present a predicted category as knowledge of an internal state.

In parallel, the scenario policy identifies observable features such as a specific request, maintained boundary, non-blaming statement, acknowledgement, or practical next step. It selects the next action and decides whether the exchange advances, continues under resistance, closes unresolved, reaches a defined outcome, or stops at the turn limit. A generator may phrase the selected action but cannot change it; invalid or unavailable generation invokes the template for the same action. Feedback uses the recorded features and supporting turns, with optional generation limited to restating that authoritative record.

Outside active role-play, deterministic lexical rules may select a bounded support strategy. Labels such as intent, cause, readiness, resistance, or cognitive pattern denote which authored rule fired, not validated psychological constructs. They remain contestable, have no authority over the rehearsal or research workflow, and are evaluated only for rule conformance.

The same authority order governs retries and concurrent requests. A turn is accepted only against the expected session state; a stale update cannot overwrite a newer transition. Retry and branch operations copy declared context but create distinct continuation records, so later feedback can separate inherited turns from new evidence. These constraints make replay reproducible and prevent a delayed provider response from retroactively changing a dialogue decision that has already been persisted.

\section{Ethics, Privacy, and Design Limitations}
\label{sec:ethical-considerations-limitations}

AffectLab is intended for voluntary adult rehearsal of ordinary interpersonal conversations. It is not a diagnostic, therapeutic, crisis-monitoring, employment-assessment, educational-assessment, or surveillance system. Completing a scenario means satisfying its declared simulated conditions; it does not establish that the response is appropriate in every real relationship or that the user has improved a general communication skill.

The most important ethical constraint is that annotated expression is not direct access to emotion. IEMOCAP contains ten actors in controlled English-language interactions, and its categories simplify ambiguous affective experience \cite{busso2008iemocap}. Speaker-independent evaluation limits identity leakage but does not establish demographic, cultural, device, or deployment validity. Confidence and text--audio agreement also do not prove correctness, especially under distribution shift \cite{guo2017calibration,ovadia2019shift}. The architecture therefore confines predictions to low-consequence choices, preserves a non-adaptive path, and allows the user to decline voice or adaptation without penalty.

Privacy constraints follow purpose limitation and data minimisation \cite{eu2016gdpr}. Microphone use requires an explicit action; transcripts are reviewable before submission; raw audio is not intentionally retained; conversation storage is time-bounded and account-owned; and research exports exclude direct identifiers, raw conversations, audio, feedback prose, custom scenario wording, and personal takeaways. Pseudonymous records remain potentially linkable while withdrawal is supported and must not be described as anonymous. External transcription or generation also creates a provider boundary that requires disclosure, protected credentials, and a text-only fallback.

Safety remains deliberately limited. Conservative English lexical checks can miss indirect, coded, misspelled, sarcastic, or multilingual expressions and can interrupt quoted or hypothetical language. The application does not know the user's location, provide continuous monitoring, perform clinical triage, or contact emergency services. These limits must accompany the interruption response and prevent the application from being presented as a substitute for professional support.

Fairness and accessibility also shape the design. Text must remain a complete route through the application so that an accent, transcription error, unavailable microphone, or preference not to speak does not remove core functionality. Keyboard operation, visible focus, labelled controls, status announcements, reduced-motion support, and responsive layouts are architectural requirements rather than interface polish. Even so, aggregate model scores and automated interface checks cannot establish equitable performance or accessibility across cultures, communication styles, disabilities, and devices; representative evaluation remains necessary.

These safeguards are design constraints, not evidence of legal compliance. Processing purpose, institutional role, jurisdiction, provider terms, and recipients of affect outputs can change the applicable obligations. In particular, voluntary rehearsal does not authorise employers or educational institutions to use individual emotion inferences for assessment or discipline \cite{eu2024aiact}. Any such change would require a new ethical and legal analysis rather than reuse of the present evaluation.

Deterministic dialogue and feedback improve reproducibility but recognise only anticipated states and language patterns; valid paraphrases, indirect communication, and culturally specific wording may be missed. Generative wording remains probabilistic despite validation. Accessibility checks cannot establish usability for every disabled user, and technical safeguards cannot guarantee confidentiality in an incorrectly configured or compromised deployment. The principal empirical limitations---benchmark validity, authored test coverage, local performance scope, and the non-adaptive pilot---are consolidated with the results in Chapter~\ref{chap5}. Accordingly, deployment still requires target-environment security, privacy, accessibility, provider, model, and legal review; the dissertation does not claim compliance certification.
